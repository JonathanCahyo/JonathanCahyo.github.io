SMARTY: a small language model that answers questions about company data, offline

A desktop assistant for workforce, unit-cost, and productivity questions. The model never computes anything: it chooses a tool (SQL, Excel/CSV, or PDF reader), and Python does the maths deterministically. That keeps sensitive data on the machine and keeps the answers checkable.

  1. 1Question in plain language
  2. 2SLM picks a tool and its arguments
  3. 3SQL · Excel/CSV · PDF tool runs
  4. 4Python computes the result
  5. 5Answer with its source
  • Compared three 4-bit quantised models (Qwen3.5-4B, Gemma 4 E4B-IT, Phi-4-mini-instruct) on 100 held-out tasks, scoring task accuracy, tool selection, response quality, latency, and resource use.
  • Built the evaluation pipeline: a resumable runner, automated scoring, SQLite logging, and 43 black-box security tests on the tools.

Grading proof-of-delivery photos for missing-parcel claims

When a customer claims a parcel never arrived, someone has to judge whether the courier's delivery photo is good enough evidence. I built a classifier that rates each photo on a five-level quality scale, plus a dashboard for the team that reviews claims.

  • Worked from more than 12,000 delivery records and hand-labelled about 1,500 photos by evidence clarity and package visibility.
  • Pipeline: aspect-ratio padding, normalisation, augmentation, transfer learning on EfficientNet-B0, adaptive learning-rate scheduling, and early stopping to hold back overfitting on a small labelled set.
  • Wrapped the model in a Streamlit dashboard so reviewers see the rating next to the photo.

Camera-trap wildlife classification: ResNet-50 against a Vision Transformer

Eight classes, more than 10,000 night-and-day camera-trap images, and one question: is the newer architecture worth its compute? On this dataset it was not.

Accuracy86.77%82.23%

  • Resizing, normalisation, and augmentation to make the two models comparable on identical inputs.
  • The confusion matrix shows where the errors live: empty frames mistaken for small animals, and antelope confused with monkeys in poor light.

Early diabetes-risk prediction, from model comparison to a usable app

Compared Random Forest, AdaBoost, and LightGBM on more than 100,000 patient records, tuned them with randomised search, then shipped the best one as a web form anyone can fill in.

  • LightGBM reached the highest accuracy at 98.56%, ahead of both Random Forest and AdaBoost.
  • Weighed predictive performance against compute cost, not accuracy alone.
  • Deployed through Streamlit: eight inputs, one prediction.
